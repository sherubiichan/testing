Welcome to planet Earth! (Who are you?) ...I am a representation of a human woman, a carbon-based life form made up of primarily water, with intricate wiring making up a neurological network that responds to chemical signals. We humans typically walk upright on 2 legs, when we aren't sitting or lying down, and we're fairly fragile. We communicate primarily through vibrations that make up sounds that make up words that make up ideas that have created coordinated civilizations. More recently, we created written versions of the sounds, with symbols and letters made up of dots and lines, for knowledge we want to preserve in the physical world. If you visited Earth 30,000 rotations around the sun ago or more, rotations we call "years", humans would have looked, acted, and probably sounded and smelled quite different. If you visited Earth before 30 million years ago (30MYA), you wouldn't find anything resembling a human at all. These days, you may recognize slight differences in size, skin tone, or variations in features based on genetics, environment, and diet, but most modern humans have the same 206 major bones, including the same general skull structure. This will be important to note once we start talking about archaic, now extinct species of humans.

The way we know anything about the Earth's past is incredible in itself! But it's also important to clarify that we cannot yet confirm the factuality of each and every assumption about the past, and in fact, it's difficult enough to do so accurately in the present day.

Before any recognizable writing system (or at least a recorded one with any permanence) existed, a time we call "prehistory" that makes up the vast majority of Earth and human history, we have to make assumptions about the past's items, what they were for, and what they mean, based on our present knowledge and experiences.

Depending on when a prehistoric artifact is discovered, and by whom, the artifact can be defined entirely differently, so our assumptions, and our theories, change over time with further discoveries providing more depth of knowledge.

Something interesting about reading and writing in general is that it was limited to religious and wealthy elites for thousands of years until global literacy rose to 50% by 1950 CE (in the Common Era), so most people in history did not have access to knowledge that wasn't communicated directly to them, and new information has primarily stayed within these circles. Unfortunately, so have countless potential ideas and theories about them.

In the modern day, in the year 2026 CE at the time of writing, we have reached a point where false information is everywhere, and people are hardly reading at all, even inside educational institutions. As far as pre-human, or even pre-mammal evidence for most of the planet's history, we have to rely on fossil evidence, basing our theories of their relationships to the present on millions of bone fragments or otherwise fossilized forms of long extinct species.

Some discoveries of fossils or other artifacts have been accidental, by stumbling upon evidence on a walk through nature, or during development of homes and infrastructure. But many discoveries have been intentional, planned expeditions to locations with suspected or known presence of fossils, where a team or individual carefully digs to reach deeper layers of the earth, excavating and recording any found items found within the dirt. We call this study and practice archaeology.

Archaeology is a branch within anthropology, the study of human behavior, evolution, and diversity, which tries to supply the "why" of found objects, and to make connections between genetic and physical characteristics and behavior. Some anthropologists even study language itself, and how it shapes cultures and societies, and has done since its origin.

These lenses, combined with scientific dating methods that have changed greatly over the years, are our best human attempt at understanding the past, without having any real context for it since humans rarely live beyond 100 years old.

Every human on Earth needs to eat a combination of plants and animals basically every single day to survive. Before modern grocery stores, and before planned farming as we know it, humans had to learn what was edible, and what was not. They most likely passed this knowledge on to their kin through some kind of communication, or else they had to learn the hard way, but the ones who survived learned.

The idea of man cataloguing living things is ancient, especially when those things can be eaten or used for medicinal or other survival purposes. In fact, the earliest known medical text is a Sumerian tablet from around 4KYA (4 thousand years ago) that describes not only symptoms of a disease and diagnosis, but which plant species to use to treat the symptoms. The Sumerian and Mesopotamian people built advanced planned civilizations, with advanced planned agriculture with meticulously documented domesticated plants and animals, that supported their societies and their economy for millenia, beginning around 5.5KYA.

This idea was mirrored in ancient Egypt, where agriculture and civilization revolved around the natural cyclical flooding of the Nile River running the length of the kingdom, so domestication of plants and animals in alignment with seasonal patterns was a measured process, and highly systematized, starting as early as 12KYA. And those are likely not the oldest examples of domestication - only some of the oldest that were written down and discovered thousands of years later.

Starting with Aristotle in ancient Greece, around 384-322 years Before Common Era (BCE, or about 2400 years ago), we begin to see classification of living organisms in his book "Historia Animalium (History of Animals)" that are based on certain qualities, such as giving birth to live young or laying eggs, having blood, a warm body, and/ or four legs. Having blood would today be considered a trait of vertebrates, and not having blood would be considered an invertebrate trait. Aristotle also divided all living things into 2 groups: Plants and Animals, though his primary focus were animals.

Aristotle sought to arrange the world into a "Scala Naturae" (Natural Ladder), key to the idea of the great chain of being with the line descending from God, to angels, humans, animals, and plants, to minerals.

His student, Theophrastus, was deemed "the father of botany" - the study of plant science - after his monumental books "Historia Plantarum (Enquiry into Plants)" which noted medicinal or industrial uses alongside mapping relationships between the 500 plants based on their structure, reproduction, and growth. We still use some of his original names such as Crocus, Cornus, and Narcissus as plant Genera (plural of Genus - though the term and formal idea wouldn't solidify for around 2000 years).

Joseph Pitton de Tournefort, a French botanist, sought to reduce every species to its Genus, and formally defined the term "Genus" as a grouping category for species above the species level, but below the Family in his book Élémens de botanique ou methode pour connaître les plantes in French in 1694, and in Latin under the name Institutiones rei herbariae in 1700, including more than 9,000 species in 698 genera.

While he wasn't the first to come up with any of his ideas, it was simply the most comprehensive and readable text on the subject, he is still considered "the founder of the modern concept of genera".

The term "Genus" comes from the Proto-Indo-European root "genh/gene": "to give birth" or "beget." In Latin, this root became the noun genus (stem: gener-), meaning "race," "stock," or "kind."

The first person to try to categorize species into standardized groups based on physical appearance of fossils was Carl Linnaeus, a Swedish botanist who published his book, Systema Naturae, Latin for "A General System of Nature", a little under 300 years ago. He is considered the "father of modern taxonomy": the science of naming of biological organisms based on shared characteristics.

He famously said, "If you do not know the names of things, the knowledge of them is lost, too."

He became ennobled by King Adolf Frederick of Sweden in 1757 CE (confirmed in 1761 CE) for his contributions to science, thereafter becoming known as Carl von Linné.

He formalized the usage of binomial nomenclature, a system of scientific naming where the Genus is written first, and the species second. For example, modern humans are called Homo sapiens. Homo is the genus, and sapiens is the species. Sort of like how many humans write their names: A family name/ surname/ "last" name, and a given name/ "first" name, in whatever order is customary. In Carl Linnaeus's case, his given name is Carl, and his family name is Linnaeus. Instead of repeating someone's full name each time we reference them, we typically use their family name.

Linnaeus also created the "nested" or ranked hierarchy of classification: Kingdoms, Classes, Orders, Genera, and Species. Again, for modern humans, this would be: Animalia, Mammalia, Primates, Homo, sapiens. However, like shortening a person's full name to their family name, when speaking of non-human species, we typically reference them only by Genus and species.

Linnaeus was the first human to suggest humans were related to Primates, or any animal at all - humans at the time preferred to believe they were different from, perhaps above, animals. The idea started to catch on in the scientific community over 100 years later, as more research was done, and further explorations of humanity's past had been done.
However, Linnaeus tried to systematically divide species so cleanly that he proposed 4 "varieties" of modern humans, based upon location and physical characteristics like skin and hair color, alongside thin stereotypical descriptions of behavior. Critics say he fuelled the division of humans based upon a manufactured idea of scientific race, though Linnaeus himself noted the differences were not enough to make them distinct species, or even "subspecies".

The first to try to make standardized evolutionary family trees, based on Linnaeus's work but still 100 years before genetic testing, were American geologist Edward Hitchcock in 1840, Charles Darwin in 1859, and Ernst Haeckel in 1866.

Hitchcock published Elementary Geology, featuring the first paleontology charts shaped like trees depicting distinct plant and animal lineages.

Later, Charles Darwin popularized the concept for evolutionary theory, sketching the first theoretical evolutionary tree in On the Origin of Species, illustrating common descent.

Ernst Haeckel drew the first comprehensive evolutionary tree of life encompassing animals, plants, and protists - microorganisms.

Every living thing on Earth, from bacteria and fungi, to plants, to animals and humans, is believed to have shared an ancestor - the Last Universal Common Ancestor, or LUCA, about 4.2BYA, around 300 million years after Earth formed.

This belief is attributed to the shared genetic code, using DNA to encode RNA through transcription, and using ATP for energy.

Our LUCA was likely a single-celled organism that came from hydrothermal vents in the deep oceans. It wasn't the earliest form of life, but it was the root of life as we know it.
The most abundant organism on the planet - Prochlorococcus - up to 100 million cells in a single litre of water in some locations! Humans are not the main characters.

310MYA The earliest ancestors of mammals, known as synapsids, split from the reptilian and avian lineages.
Though only fragments have been discovered thus far, Protoclepsydrops (315MYA) may have been the earliest synapsid, but more fragments must be found and further research must be done for proper classification (Clepsydrops rendering shown for example)
Echinerpeton is one of the earliest discovered undisputed species in the synapsid lineage (308MYA in Nova Scotia, Canada)
Archaeothyris is another one of the earliest synapsids discovered (306MYA in Nova Scotia, Canada)
Therapsids, the only living subclade of Synapsida (including living mammals), split from their synapsid ancestors around 275MYA
Therapsids split into multiple extinct lineages alongside Theriodontia, which split into 2 major groups: Gorgonopsians (270-252MYA - extinct) and Eutheriodonts (about 266MYA)
Eutheriodonts split into 2 lineages, or potentially one split from the other: Therocephalia (265-245MYA - extinct) and Cynodontia (about 260MYA)
The Epicynodontia (259MYA) split into several lineages, including Eucynodontia (251MYA) and extinct clades
The Eucynodontia (251MYA) split into 2 major groups: Cynognathia (251-208.5MYA - extinct) and Probainognathia (235-221.5MYA)
The MSX2 gene began appearing and evolving around 240MYA, showing physically as early whiskers for sensing, as well as early body hair and fur
Prozostrodontia (233.23MYA) was the lineage that split into Mammaliamorpha (232MYA) and their closest extinct relatives
Recreation of fur-covered Prozostrodontian cynodonts Brasilodon (L - about 225.42MYA) and Riograndia (R - about 225.42MYA; both from Brazil). Brasilodon is the oldest known vertebrate to have 2 sets of teeth - milk teeth and adult teeth (diphyodonty) - an exclusive trait to mammals with lactation and suckling, and otherwise associated with endotherms (warm-blooded creatures).
Mammaliamorpha split into Mammaliaformes (227MYA) and their closest extinct relatives
Somewhere between 233-200MYA, warm blood evolved as a common trait of Mammalia, as well as in the ancestors of some dinosaurs and birds
Rendering of Morganucodon oehleri (205MYA)
Mammaliaformes split into Mammalia and its closest living (Monotremata - egg laying mammals (around 130-126MYA), and Theria - giving birth to live young with no shell (around 160MYA)), and extinct relatives somewhere between 225 and 167MYA
Theria split into 2 existing clades: Eutheria (Pan-Placentalia - 67.3MYA) and Metatheria (125MYA (Marsupials), with the evolutionary split beginning 125-160MYA. Eutheria means "true beasts" in Ancient Greek.
Castorocauda (164MYA) were semi-aquatic otter-like Mammaliaformes covered in fur
Juramaia were Therians, possibly the earliest Eutherians (about 160MYA)
Haldanodon were docodont (extinct clade) Mammaliaformes (about 145-155MYA - fossil found in modern Portugal)
Sinodelphys was initially thought to be the earliest known Metatherian, but later studies placed it as a Eutherian (125MYA - discovered in modern Liaoning Province, China)
The subdivision of Mammalia, Placentalia, contains 2 magnorders that exist to the modern day: Atlantogenata (84-97MYA) and Boreoeutheria (66.043MYA)
Atlantogenata contains the supergroups Xenarthra (60MYA) and Afrotheria (65MYA)
Boreoeutheria contains the supergroups Euarchontoglires (85-95MYA) and Laurasiatheria(66.3MYA)
Euarchontoglires is a superorder of placental mammals in the following groups: glires (Rodentia/ rodents, Lagomorpha/ lagomorphs, possibly Scandentia/ treeshrews), Latin for "dormice", and euarchonta (Primate/primates, 69.8MYA, and possibly Dermoptera/colugos), Ancient Greek for "true rulers." Euarchonta can also be referred to as Primatomorpha in cases where Scandentia are considered reclassified as glires.
Humans are thought to be descended from the Primates clade, originating from the extinct Plesiadapiformes order (66–46.2MYA), later called Pan-Primates/Primatomorpha (65.9MYA).
Plesiadapiformes included the extinct orders Purgitoriidae (like Purgatorius sp., 65.942–63MYA), Micromomyidae (56.8–50.3MYA), Carpolestidae (61.7-50.3MYA), alongside the "crown Primatatomorpha," including the orders of Dermoptera (37MYA), and the Primates (65.9MYA), which includes the suborders Strepsirrhini ("wet-snout tip" - lemurs and lorises) and Haplorrhini ("simple/dry-snout tip" - tarsiers and monkeys & apes).
Haplorrhini include the existing Tarsiiformes (56MYA) infraorder, alongside the Simiiformes/ Simian (40MYA) infraorder
Simiformes includes extinct families like Amphipithecidae and Eosimiidae, alongside existing parvorders Platyrrhini/ New World Monkeys (31MYA) and Catarrhini (35MYA, potentially split 40-44MYA). Though there is a potential for the African simian origin story based on limited fossils of Algeripithecus, the current undisputed oldest simian fossil family/ genus recognized was Eosimiidae (45-30MYA), found in modern day Liyang, southern Jiangsu province, China
Catarrhini contains the supergroup of Cercopithecoidae/ Old World Monkeys (30MYA) and the Hominoidae (at least 25MYA), as well as numerous superfamilies of extinct relatives (32-12MYA).
Proconsul is considered one of the earliest Hominoidae (28.4MYA-11.608MYA), though it is undetermined if it predated the genetic split between the Old World Monkeys and Hominoidae
Hominoidae contains the Hylobatidae/ gibbons (13.8MYA) and Hominidae (17MYA)
Hominidae contains the Ponginae (18.5MYA) and the Homininae (12.5MYA) subfamilies.
Homininae contains the tribes Gorillini (8MYA), representing gorillas and their extinct ape relatives, and Hominini (7MYA).
Hominini includes the subtribe of all hominids (Australopithecina/Hominina - 7MYA), as well as Panina/chimpanzees and bonobos (7MYA), connecting the subtribes through their currently unconfirmed Last Common Ancestor (LCA).
Australopithecina/Hominina includes Australopithecus (4.3-1.9/1.2MYA), Kenyanthropus (3.3-3.2MYA), Paranthropus (2.9-1.2MYA), and the other potential extinct Homo species. It also includes potential further back ancestor Sahelanthropus tchadensis (7-6MYA), which was the earliest to display bipedalism (walking upright on 2 legs). This example is "Mrs. Ples", an Australopithecus africanus from modern day southern Africa, 2.25MYA.
Evidence of bipedalism marked in history at the Laetoli site in the Enduleni ward of Ngorongoro District in Arusha Region, Tanzania. Hominina footprints, preserved in volcanic ash. The site of the Laetoli footprints (Site G) is located 45 km south of Olduvai Gorge. (3.7MYA)
Predating the official Homo lineage, the oldest potential ancestor to start making and using tools (called "Lomekwi/Lomekwian" tools) came about at least 3.3MYA in modern day West Turkana, Kenya

Australopithecus africanus from South Africa (3.3-2.1MYA), example: Taung child (3 year old from 2.8MYA)
The oldest artifact we can consider to be early human "art" also predated the first Homo species - the Makapansgat pebble (around 3MYA - Makapan Valley, Limpopo province, southern Africa) was a small piece of jasper that vaguely resembled a face with 2 eyes and a nose or mouth. It was not seemingly modified in any way, but considered the first human art because it was transported some 3-20 miles (5-30km) from the area where the jasper could have naturally came from, implying it was transported there by a human - possibly an Australopithecus.
The Homo genus first appeared in the fossil record around 2.8MYA, though it is still undetermined which Australopithecus species or mixture initially gave rise to it. Under the Homo genus, it is also undetermined which exact species or mixture gave rise to Homo sapiens, so there are multiple theories based on fossil evidence and their locations and estimated dating.
Homo habilis was the earliest species we have so far positively identified as being in the hominid evolutionary line, living from about 2.4MYA to about 1.4MYA
Homo habilis is one of the hominid species associated with making and using "Oldowan" tools (tools dated to about 2.9MYA-1.7MYA), named after the first place they were excavated in Olduvai Gorge, Tanzania, though they began to be produced before the earliest Homo species, possibly linking them to Australopithecus sp. before Homo habilis. These quartz and basalt artifacts include simple hammerstones, sharp stone flakes for cutting, and heavy "choppers" used for cracking animal bones.
Homo erectus is still considered the first hominid species to emerge en masse from Africa starting at least 2MYA - reaching from modern-day Spain & Portugal and south England to the northwest, to East Asia and Indonesia to the east. Their fossils have been found as late as 110KYA before disappearing.
Findings of skulls showing traits of Homo habilis and Homo erectus (called Homo erectus georgicus) from 1.8MYA in Dmanisi, Georgia, in the Caucasus Mountains, along with over 10,000 Oldowan (and later, more advanced) stone tools, challenge the popular theory that Homo erectus was the first hominid to leave Africa
Also at Dmanisi, based on the finding of a skull of an individual who had lost all but 1 of their teeth several years prior to their passing, combined with the assumed lack of fire usage for cooking food at this time, researchers argue that the individual could not have lived as long unless there was some sense of social cooperation, and potentially caretaking.
An excavation at Ubeidiya in the Jordan Rift Valley uncovered a juvenile lumbar vertebra of an early Homo species from 1.2-1.6MYA
Homo erectus is defined by a partial type specimen of the "Java Man" (around 1.49-0.7MYA) including a full skullcap and a femur bone, giving rise to the "Out of Asia" theory of human evolution
The above illustration is a 1922 reconstruction based on the Java Man
Reconstruction of the "Peking Man," a subspecies of Homo erectus called Homo erectus pekinensis (0.8-0.25MYA), based off of remains from roughly 40 individuals, including 6 nearly complete skullcaps, 12 skull fragments, 15 lower jaws (mandibles), 157 teeth, and various broken limb bones (femurs, shins, and upper arms)
Homo erectus is credited with making some of the earliest human art - the Trinil shell engravings (between 540-430KYA - Java, Indonesia), though some criticize its classification as "art"

Homo sapiens in our modern physical form first appeared around 300-315KYA in Africa

The earliest findings of Homo sapiens fossil specimens so far were excavated in Jebel Irhoud, Morocco (315KYA)

The earliest findings of Homo sapiens fossils outside of Africa so far were excavated in the Levant, in the Misliya Cave of Mount Carmel, thought to be between 177 and 194,000 years old

Another of the earliest examples of human art is a set of juvenile Homo hand and foot prints dated to around 169-229KYA that became lithified (turned to stone) at a hot spring at Quesang on the Tibetan plateau. Critics point out the lack of evidence for intentionally creating the "art," but some defend it due to the fact that the prints don't overlap much, so all prints can be seen clearly within the given space.
